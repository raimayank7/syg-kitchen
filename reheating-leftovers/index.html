How to cool, store and reheat leftovers safely

Source and date. The temperatures and times on this page are those published by the Food Safety and Inspection Service of the United States Department of Agriculture (USDA FSIS) on its pages "Leftovers and Food Safety" (last updated 31 July 2020) and "Safe Minimum Internal Temperature Chart" (last updated 14 April 2025), read on 11 November 2026. Celsius figures are our conversion of the published Fahrenheit figures. In India, follow the advice of FSSAI and the instructions on food packets. This is general information, not medical advice. People who are pregnant, elderly, very young or unwell should take extra care.

Leftovers are part of everyday Indian cooking: last night's dal, extra rice, a curry made in bulk for two days. Handled well, they are safe and often tastier. Handled carelessly, they are a common cause of stomach upsets. The official guidance comes down to a handful of numbers and habits.

The numbers to remember

  • The danger zone is 40 °F to 140 °F (about 4 °C to 60 °C). The guidance says bacteria grow rapidly between these temperatures. An Indian kitchen is inside this zone for most of the year.
  • Two hours. Leftovers must be refrigerated within 2 hours of cooking, or of being taken off the heat that was keeping them hot. Perishable food left at room temperature for longer should be thrown away.
  • One hour when it is hot. The limit drops to 1 hour when the temperature is above 90 °F (about 32 °C). In much of India that describes an ordinary summer day, and many kitchens for most of the year.
  • Fridge at 40 °F (about 4 °C) or below.
  • 3 to 4 days in the fridge, or 3 to 4 months in the freezer for best quality. Frozen leftovers stay safe indefinitely but slowly lose moisture and flavour.
  • Reheat to 165 °F (about 74 °C), measured with a food thermometer. Bring sauces, soups and gravies to a rolling boil.

Cooling food quickly

The aim is to bring food down to fridge temperature as fast as possible. The guidance recommends:

  • Divide large amounts into shallow containers. A big pot of soup, or dal, takes a long time to cool in the middle.
  • Cut large pieces of food into smaller portions.
  • Hot food can be placed directly in the refrigerator, or chilled first in an ice or cold water bath. You do not have to wait for it to reach room temperature, and in a hot kitchen waiting is the risk.

In practice: when dinner is over, move what is left into flat steel or glass boxes, cover them, and put them in the fridge before washing up, not after.

Covering and storing

Cover leftovers, wrap them or seal them in storage containers. The guidance notes that this keeps bacteria out, holds in moisture and stops food picking up smells from the rest of the fridge. Label the box with the day if your household tends to forget what was cooked when.

Thawing frozen leftovers

The guidance lists three safe ways, each with a condition:

  • In the refrigerator. The slowest way, and the food stays safe throughout. Use within 3 to 4 days, or refreeze.
  • In cold water. Faster. The food must be in a leak-proof pack or bag. Food thawed this way should be cooked before it is refrozen.
  • In the microwave. The fastest. Continue heating until the food reaches 165 °F (about 74 °C). It can be refrozen after heating to that temperature.

Thawing on the kitchen counter is not on the list. It is also safe to reheat frozen leftovers without thawing, in a pan, an oven or a microwave. It simply takes longer.

Reheating properly

  • Dal, sambar, rasam, kadhi, curries with gravy: bring to a rolling boil, stirring so that the bottom does not catch. A lukewarm dal is not reheated.
  • Dry sabzi, rice, pulao, biryani: sprinkle with a little water, cover and heat until steaming hot right through. The guidance says covering holds in moisture and helps food heat all the way through.
  • In a microwave: spread the food evenly in a microwave-safe glass or ceramic dish, add a little liquid if needed, cover with a vented lid and stir or rotate partway. Microwaves leave cold spots, so let the food stand briefly and check more than one place.

Few home kitchens in India own a food thermometer. The published standard is the 165 °F reading. Without a thermometer, "boiling" for liquids and "steaming hot in the centre, too hot to touch" for solids is the practical test, and a thermometer is the only way to be certain.

Take out only what you will eat

Reheat the portion for this meal, not the whole box. The guidance does allow refreezing what remains after previously frozen leftovers have been reheated to 165 °F, and allows thawing a large container in the fridge, taking a portion and refreezing the rest. But each trip through the danger zone lowers quality and adds risk, so smaller boxes are the simpler habit.

Applying it to an Indian kitchen

  • Cooked rice is perishable food like any other. Do not leave it on the counter overnight. Refrigerate it within the time limit and reheat until steaming.
  • Dough, cut fruit, chutneys with fresh coconut, raita and anything with curd, milk, paneer or cream belong in the fridge between uses.
  • Lunch boxes packed in the morning and eaten at one o'clock spend hours in the danger zone. Pack food either cold from the fridge or piping hot in an insulated container.
  • Functions and buffets: the guidance says to keep hot food at 140 °F (60 °C) or warmer, in chafing dishes or warming trays, and cold food at 40 °F (about 4 °C) or below, for example on ice.
  • Power cuts: keep the fridge door shut. If you are unsure how long food has been warm, do not taste it to find out.

When to throw food away

Throw it away if it has been out beyond the time limit, if it has been in the fridge for more than 3 to 4 days, or if you cannot remember when it was cooked. Smell and appearance are not reliable tests: the USDA chart page notes that harmful bacteria cannot be seen, smelt or tasted. Storage times for raw meat, eggs and other foods are on our food storage page, and safe cooking temperatures are on the temperature chart.

Questions people ask

How long can cooked food be left out?

USDA guidance says no more than 2 hours at room temperature, and no more than 1 hour when the temperature is above 90 °F (about 32 °C).

Can I put hot food straight into the fridge?

Yes. The USDA guidance says hot food can be placed directly in the refrigerator, or cooled quickly in an ice or cold water bath first. Use shallow containers.

How long do leftovers last in the fridge?

3 to 4 days according to USDA guidance, or 3 to 4 months in the freezer for best quality.

What temperature should leftovers be reheated to?

165 °F, about 74 °C, measured with a food thermometer. Sauces, soups and gravies should be brought to a rolling boil.
